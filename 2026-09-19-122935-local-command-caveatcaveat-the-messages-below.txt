  The one idea

  A System 1 decision model: a bidirectional encoder that answers typed
  questions about a state in a single non-autoregressive forward pass, returning
  calibrated probabilities instead of text. Three primitives — choice (pick a
  key), score (ordinal rubric level), noul (P(true)).

  The trick that makes arbitrary runtime questions possible is the [MASK] marker
  mechanism (rl_common.py:47, mirrored at laya/common.py:35):

  [CLS] <type> question: <instructions> [SEP] [MASK] opt0 [MASK] opt1 ... [SEP]
  <state> [SEP]

  One [MASK] per option. torch.gather pulls hidden states at those marker
  positions → a shared scalar scorer MLP → one logit per option → softmax.
  Because options live in the sequence, the answer space is defined at inference
  time; no fixed label head, no retraining for new schemas. noul is always
  rendered [false, true] so p[1] is the probability by construction.

  RLCD — why RL and not cross-entropy

  proper_reward (rl_common.py:138) is the heart:

  r = Σ y·log q  +  w_sph · (Σ y·q)/‖q‖  −  w_rps · RPS   [ordinal only]

  All three components are strictly proper scoring rules, so the reward is
  uniquely maximized by honest probabilities. Standard CE pushes the winning
  logit to infinity (overconfidence); binary-reward RL pushes p→1.0 (destroys
  calibration). Proper scoring is the fix.

  The update (rl_loss, train.py:371) is GRPO-style REINFORCE:
  1. Sample G noisy logit vectors: ε ~ N(0, σ²), then zero-mean-projected across
     options (train.py:390) — softmax ignores constant shifts, so that
     direction is pure wasted variance.
  2. Score each sample with proper_reward, advantage = group-mean-baselined +
     std-normalized.
  3. Gaussian log-prob logp = -‖z-logits‖²/2σ², loss = -(adv · logp).mean().
  4. σ anneals sigma_start → sigma_end over training (explore → exploit).

  Two extras: an act/escalate head trained with its own cost matrix
  (cost_wrong_act: 3.0, act_costs.escalate: 0.5 ⇒ acting pays only when
  P(correct) > 0.625), and TD(λ) targets for sales-conversation prefixes. The
  config comment at default_config.py:52 records that λ<1.0 bootstrapping
  collapsed to a flat 0.5 in the first full run — hence λ=1.0 (pure Monte
  Carlo).

  Data pipeline

  prepare_data.py — 24 streaming HF sources → Jev-shaped typed questions.
  Notable engineering:
  - Byte budget enforced by a thread-safe Budget guard, not post-hoc pruning.
  - Decontamination: eval splits stream first, their state hashes go in a set,
    train records matching a hash are dropped (:95).
  - Held-out zero-shot sources (sst5_zs, emotion_zs, prompt_injections_zs) plus
    ~15% of natural_instructions tasks held out by task hash — real
    generalization measurement.
  - Adversarial jaggedness source (_jag_record, :570): code-generated,
    exactly-labeled
    counting/date-ordering/double-negation/prompt-injection-resistance tasks,
    targeting documented failure modes.
  - Question phrasings, option orders, and state serialization (JSON vs prose)
    are all randomized per-record, so the model can't memorize surface form.

  Batching is token-budgeted (make_token_batches + pack_groups), and pack_groups
  re-packs on real tokenized lengths because the index only stores estimates.

⏺ laya-multilingual: max_len = 1024 tokens. That's the effective limit —
  build_sequence hard-truncates at ids[:max_len].

  That 1024 splits into two budgets:

  ┌───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┬────────┐
  │                                                       │ tokens │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼────────┤
  │ head_max_len — question text + [MASK] + option labels │ 256    │
  ├───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┼────────┤
  │ remainder for your state                              │ ~768   │
  └───────────────────────────────────────────────────────┴────────┘

  So a single question with few options gives you roughly 768 tokens of state; a
  20-option question consumes the full 256-token head and still leaves ~768.
  Options are truncated to fit before the state is, so a large option set costs
  you instructions detail, not state.

  Two things worth knowing:

  The architectural ceiling is 8192, not 1024. The mmBERT-base encoder has
  max_position_embeddings: 8192 with alternating local (128-token window) /
  global attention every 3rd layer. You can raise it at inference:

  agent = laya.load("convaiinnovations/laya-multilingual")
  agent.cfg["max_len"] = 4096          # up to 8192
  agent.cfg["head_max_len"] = 512      # optional, more room for options

  Nothing will error. But it was trained at 1024 (rl_agent_config.json from a
  run whose notebook set MAX_LEN = 1024), so positions beyond that are
  extrapolation — expect degradation, and measure it rather than trusting it.
  Attention cost also grows fast past the point where global layers dominate.

  For comparison, laya (English) is capped at 512 — half the context, and only
  ~320 tokens of state after its 192-token head budget. That's a real asymmetry
  between the two checkpoints and one reason I added the matched-512 control
  cell to the benchmark notebook: on long states, any multilingual win could
  partly be the bigger window rather than the model.

  Practical note: because mmBERT tokenizes non-Latin scripts efficiently
  (Japanese was ~0.6 tokens/char, Hindi ~0.5, Tamil ~0.4 in my check), 768 state
  tokens goes considerably further on CJK/Indic text than the raw number
  suggests.

  What's actually good: cross-lingual NLI

  XNLI 0.738 macro across 15 languages is the real result. English 0.843, and
  the drop-off is gentle and sensible — Spanish/Bulgarian/German 0.79, Chinese
  0.76, Arabic/Greek 0.73, Thai 0.70, Urdu 0.68, Hindi 0.66, Swahili 0.63. No
  script collapse anywhere.

  For context, published zero-shot cross-lingual transfer on XNLI (train
  English, test 15 languages) typically lands around the mid-70s for XLM-R base
  and near 80 for XLM-R large. So a 322M model reaching 0.738 is roughly
  XLM-R-base territory — respectable, not embarrassing. It's also the family
  with by far the best calibration (ECE 0.181, and 0.086 on English).

  This is the one place I'd say the multilingual claim is earned.

  What's mediocre: intent/scenario routing

  0.466 and 0.447 look terrible next to the 9× random headroom, but the absolute
  number is what matters for deployment. At ~47% on a 20-way choice you can use
  it to narrow candidates or to route with a human fallback; you cannot use it
  to auto-route. Fine-tuned multilingual encoders on MASSIVE reach the mid-80s,
  so there's a very large gap that fine-tuning would close.

  Language ordering is sensible — Chinese 0.607, Japanese 0.587, French 0.553
  down to Tamil 0.250 and Swahili 0.210. Low-resource languages are weak but not
  broken the way laya is (Swahili 0.103, i.e. 2× random).

  What's broken

  Three results that matter more than the averages:

  - typed-decisions 0.351 vs 0.318 random — 1.1× random, below the 0.461
    majority-class baseline. No usable signal on those four workflows.
  - SST-5 0.282 vs 0.200 random, with ECE 0.523 and mean confidence 0.805. It is
    28% accurate while reporting 80% confidence. Ordinal sentiment is
    effectively non-functional.
  - prompt-injections 0.578 vs 0.500 random, mean confidence 0.940. This one is
    the most consequential: guardrails are a headline use case and a demo tab,
    and the model is near-coin-flip while sounding certain.

  The pattern is clean: it performs on tasks in its training mix (ag_news 0.937,
  BoolQ 0.787, NLI) and falls apart on genuinely held-out ones (sst5,
  prompt_injections, emotion 0.513). That's memorised task coverage, not
  generalisation.

  Systematic overconfidence

  Mean confidence sits at 0.75–0.97 across nearly every suite while accuracy is
  far below. That's expected — it shipped with temperature = [1.0, 1.0, 1.0] and
  no option-count buckets, i.e. never calibrated at all.

  The fix is cheap and large: refitting temperature out-of-sample takes mean ECE
  0.314 → 0.106, and on individual suites 0.523 → 0.031 (sst5), 0.314 → 0.053
  (typed-decisions). Nothing about the model needs to change.

  Speed is a real strength

  32.8 ms for one question, 72.3 ms for ten, 6.75 ms/question at fifty — about
  2× faster than laya and 4–10× faster than Jev's measured 264–710 ms. Batched
  throughput reaches 332 q/s on a T4. For a 322M model that's a solid
  engineering result, and it's the least contestable claim in the whole set.

  One weakness: option-order flip rate of 0.23 on 20-option intent (vs laya
  0.15, Jev 0.13). It changes its answer on nearly a quarter of option
  permutations.

  Bottom line

  It is a competent multilingual NLI/entailment model with fast inference, 
  wrapped in claims it doesn't support. The multilingual transfer is real —
  mmBERT was the right encoder choice, and it fixes laya's total collapse on
  non-Latin scripts. But outside NLI and its trained task set, accuracy is low
  and confidence is uninformative, which undercuts the "calibrated decisions you
  can branch on" pitch specifically.

  Three things would move it most, in order:
  1. Ship fitted temperatures — free, cuts ECE 3×.
  2. Don't market it for guardrails or rubric scoring until prompt-injection and
     ordinal-score performance improve; both are at/near chance.
  3. Fine-tune per workflow. typed-decisions went 0.35 → 0.766 with fine-tuning.
     That's the actual product story: a fast multilingual base you fine-tune,
     not a zero-shot decision engine.
